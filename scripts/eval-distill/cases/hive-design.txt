**The patterns worth stealing from**

*Muse (Meta)* — the layering and the claim model.

*Karpathy's LLM Wiki* — compiled knowledge instead of per-query RAG. Raw immutable, derived pages source-cited, a schema that constrains the agent, and a **lint** pass: orphans, contradictions, stale pages, low-confidence single-source claims. Human-readable, diff-able, git-able.

- **Pages** — compiled from *promoted* claims only, source-cited markdown per entity. What the bot actually reads and cites. Backstage-shaped for systems.
- **Index** — pgvector + full-text over claims and pages, every query filtered by the caller's entitlements. Hybrid search → rerank → context block, per Zep.
- **Synthesis** — nightly, per domain: contradiction report, stale sweep (re-verify systems against source of truth), new-candidate digest, promotion queue. This is what lands in the owner's Slack DM in the morning — the curators read the synthesis, not the firehose.
- **Forgetting** — Muse's cascading retraction for corrections and disputes; bi-temporal expiry (not delete) so history survives; hard delete only for legal/privacy requests.

**Where multi-writer departs from Muse**
- Identity on every claim, and personal-tier claims never leave the user's scope without an explicit promotion act.
- Conflict detection is *cross-user*: Jon's bot learned the rate limit is 100, Priya's learned 500 → both flagged, owner asked. Muse never had to handle two people being wrong differently.
- Retrieval is entitlement-filtered; a `lesson` learned in the payments team isn't automatically visible to marketing's bot.

**One idea I want to float before we go further: promotion to enterprise tier as a pull request.**
Render pages to a `hive` git repo. Promotion = a PR; `CODEOWNERS` maps domains to owning teams — which *is* federated governance, using plumbing every engineering org already runs and audits. History, revert, blame, and review all come free. Non-engineers never see git: the Slack "Promote" button opens and approves the PR behind the scenes. Postgres stays source of truth for claims (concurrency, temporal queries need a DB — this is where files-only breaks); the repo is the reviewed, human-legible projection. I think that collapses a lot of the admin UI we'd otherwise build.

**The problem we're solving**
Lots of people will each have their own bot. Each bot learns things. We want one shared "company brain" that all bots read from and contribute to — but we don't want it filling up with junk, wrong facts, or one person's private stuff.

**How the company brain is organized — think of it as a newsroom**

- **Raw notes** — everything that happened: conversations, things a pipeline built, documents we pulled in. Never edited. This is the evidence.
- **Claims** — small individual facts pulled out of the raw notes. "The Customer API is owned by the Payments team." "Deploys to Cloud Run take about 4 minutes." Each claim records *who said it, where, when, and how sure we are*. This is the thing we approve, reject, or correct — one fact at a time, not whole documents.
- **Published pages** — a clean, readable page per system, tool, decision, or term, written only from *approved* claims. This is what the bots actually read and quote from.
- **Search** — so a bot can find the relevant pages for whatever someone just asked.
- **The nightly editor** — a job that runs overnight and writes up: new facts waiting for approval, two facts that contradict each other, facts that look out of date. Owners get a short morning digest in Slack instead of having to watch everything.

**Three shelves**
- **Personal** — your bot's notes about you and your work. Nobody else sees these.
- **Team** — things your team has agreed are true.
- **Company** — things the whole company can rely on.
A fact only moves up a shelf when someone approves it. Facts pulled straight from a source of truth (a repo, a deploy record) can skip the approval because they aren't anyone's opinion.

**What "approve" looks like day to day**
The person who owns the Payments system gets a Slack message: "Jon's bot thinks the Customer API caps at 100 requests/sec. Approve / Fix / Reject." Three taps. Same thing in reverse when someone says "that's wrong" — a dispute goes to the owner.

**Why the Muse diagram matters**
Meta's design does two things we should copy: it tracks every fact down to its source so the bot can always answer "why do you think that?", and when something is forgotten it removes *everything derived from it* so it can't creep back in. **Time matters too**
